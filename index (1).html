// Reflection

"Ninja" gave me a lot of room to work with, but I didn't want to go somewhere surface-level — black backgrounds, Japanese lettering, sword trails. I wanted to find the behavioral core of a ninja: precision, concealment, velocity. The three iterations became three distinct readings of that idea, each one committing to a different angle instead of trying to cover everything at once.

Stealth was the hardest to get right technically. I wanted the particles to be almost invisible before the mouse got close — but p5.js has a minimum visible alpha threshold, and anything too low just disappears instead of fading. It took a lot of tuning on the alpha-to-distance mapping function before I got that "barely there" feeling. The shadow waves in the background were a late addition that ended up doing a lot of atmospheric work.

Speed is the one I'm most satisfied with. The combination of speed lines and Perlin noise paths creates a sense of motion that doesn't need any label to be readable. After finishing this theme I realized that giving yourself a single keyword is actually a forcing function — you can't do everything, so you have to choose a point of entry and commit to it.

// Reflection

My starting point for "Ghost" wasn't horror — it was uncertainty of presence. Is it here? Does it know you're here? Ethereal Drift captures that most directly: the particles don't flee from the mouse, they drift away, like a ghost that isn't afraid of you and simply doesn't want to overlap with you. That distinction in behavior felt important.

Haunted Connection was the most technically complex of the three. The line brightness depends on the distance between points, and the mouse acts as an energy source that amplifies nearby connections — so there are three variables all hitting the renderer at the same time. Early versions were just visual noise. It took several rounds of rebalancing before it started to feel like a living web rather than random flickering.

Possession surprised me. The warping grid was a last-minute addition — I'd originally planned only particle attraction — but once I added the grid distortion the whole piece clicked into place. Sometimes one small visual decision changes the entire feel of something. That was a useful thing to learn.

// Reflection

Hurricane felt like the most "physical" of the three themes, so I wanted each iteration to be grounded in real mechanics rather than just visual mood. Destruction uses the mouse as the storm's eye and violently repels particles from it — the most intuitive approach. The color shift from cool to warm with distance was added later, and it's what pushes it from "particle simulation" to something that reads like weather data.

Storm Surge is where I spent the most time. Simulating layered wave surfaces, making the wave height respond to the mouse's proximity, adding whitecap behavior at the peaks — each layer is a separate sine wave stacked on top of the others, and by the end I'd lost track of which parameter controlled what. I was just running it, watching, adjusting, running it again. The iterative process was very much the process.

Wind Speed Gradient started as a data visualization exercise, but it ended up being the most aesthetically satisfying of the three. Encoding wind speed into line length is a simple idea on paper, but the visual result was better than I expected — it has the clean legibility of a weather map while still being alive and moving. I didn't plan for it to be the most beautiful one. It just happened.

THEME 04

Misrecognition

A camera prototype that makes algorithmic bias visible — showing how a system decides who is seen and who isn't.

Prototype
Conceptual Work

Misrecognition Camera

The video feed is simplified and the face-detection box flickers or disappears depending on a simulated bias level. Debug text shows the system's "thinking" in real time — how likely it is to recognize someone. Small glitches and distortions appear gradually, showing step-by-step how the camera interprets — or misinterprets — what it sees.

The goal: let users experience the process of misrecognition, not just see a final glitchy result.

Open Sketch ↗
// Reflection

The starting point was a question: can you make someone feel what it's like to be misread by an algorithm, rather than just reading about it? Facial recognition bias has been written about extensively, but most of it stays at the level of statistics and case studies. I wanted to make it into a first-person visual experience — something you feel before you analyze.

I chose a deliberately simplified "fake video feed" — scanline noise, a flickering detection box, CONF and BIAS values shifting in real time. The numbers are fictional, but the visual language comes from real systems. The goal was to produce that specific feeling of "I'm being assessed right now" — the uneasy awareness of being read by something that doesn't fully understand what it's looking at.

The hardest part wasn't technical — it was calibration. Too dramatic and it becomes sci-fi. Too quiet and the unease doesn't register. I ended up using a slow sine drift for the BIAS value and a probability function to decide whether the detection box appears on any given frame. That randomness means each run is slightly different, and the instability itself becomes part of the piece.

// Reflection

This project started differently from everything else in the course — not from an abstract concept, but from a real ongoing argument. My friends and I have never agreed on what it actually means to be a real fan, and I wanted to turn that argument into something other people could participate in. The quiz format made sense immediately: it forces a decision without letting you hedge.

Designing the five archetypes was the most interesting part of the process. The Analyst, The Loyalist, Player Follower, Hot Take Machine, Casual Superfan — none of these are invented personality types. They're descriptions of people I actually know. The questions work the same way: every scenario is a real situation I've seen play out, and the answer choices are real things real fans say and do.

Adding localStorage was a late call — I initially just wanted something that ran to completion, but then thought about what happens when someone closes the tab halfway through. It was worth adding. The detail I'm most proud of is using CSS variables to shift the entire page's accent color based on your result. It means your answer doesn't just sit in a text box — it actually changes what you're looking at.

// 03 — about

Code as
Creative Medium

These projects explore what happens when you give a word — ninja, ghost, hurricane — to a generative system and ask it to respond visually. Each theme became three sketches, each sketch a different interpretation: one about form, one about interaction, one about atmosphere.

The NBA quiz came from a different place — a community I'm actually part of, a question I actually wanted to answer: what kind of fan are you, really?
